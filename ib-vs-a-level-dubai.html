A decision that can feel bigger than it needs to

IB or A Level? Start with your child, not the label

Parents often hear strong opinions about both routes. One family will say the IB is the best preparation for university. Another will say A Levels allow a student to focus on what they are genuinely good at. Both can be true — for different children.

The useful question is not “Which one is better?” It is “Which structure is more likely to help my child do well without becoming overwhelmed?”

In simple terms

Broad versus focused

  • IB DiplomaA broad programme with six subjects plus core requirements such as the Extended Essay, Theory of Knowledge and CAS.
  • A LevelsA smaller number of subjects studied in greater depth, usually allowing more specialisation.
  • The deciding factorHow your child handles breadth, depth, deadlines, independent work and subject choice.

What the choice feels like day to day

The timetable matters more than the brochure

Two students with the same academic ability can experience these programmes very differently. One may enjoy variety and constant movement between subjects. Another may feel relieved to narrow their focus.

01

The student who likes breadth

A student who enjoys both humanities and sciences, writes well, manages deadlines and does not want to drop too many subjects may enjoy the IB structure.

02

The student who wants depth

A student who already has a clear academic direction may prefer A Levels because they can devote more time to a smaller number of chosen subjects.

03

Organisation becomes academic

In the IB, managing several subjects alongside core work is part of the challenge. A bright student who leaves everything until the last minute can find the workload emotionally draining.

04

Subject choice still needs care

A Levels may offer more focus, but the combination matters. University courses can require specific subjects, so choices should be made with likely future options in mind.

05

Neither route protects against stress

Students can become overwhelmed in either system. The warning signs are often similar: falling confidence, avoidance, late-night catch-up, slipping deadlines and the feeling that there is never enough time.

06

School support matters

A strong programme is not only the qualification on paper. Timetabling, subject teaching, pastoral support and how well a school prepares students for assessment can shape the experience enormously.

The emotional side parents sometimes miss

A capable student can still be in the wrong structure

When a previously confident teenager starts saying, “I can’t do this,” it is easy to assume they need to work harder. Sometimes they do. But sometimes the problem is the volume, the pace or the way the course is organised around them.

Watch the pattern, not one bad week

One difficult deadline does not mean the programme is wrong. A repeated pattern of exhaustion, missed work, panic and loss of confidence deserves a closer look. The aim is not to remove every challenge. It is to make sure the challenge is productive rather than crushing.

A useful family conversation

Questions worth asking before you decide

These questions usually reveal more than asking which qualification has the stronger reputation.

What subjects does your child genuinely enjoy?

Not simply which subjects produce the highest marks today, but which ones they are willing to spend time thinking about.

How do they manage competing deadlines?

Do they naturally plan ahead, or do multiple simultaneous demands quickly become stressful?

Do they know what they may want to study later?

A likely university direction can influence whether breadth or subject specialisation makes more sense.

Common questions

IB and A Levels in plain English

Is IB harder than A Level?

Not in a simple universal sense. IB can feel demanding because of its breadth and core requirements. A Levels can be demanding because students go deeper into a smaller number of subjects. The student matters more than the slogan.

Do universities prefer one?

Universities commonly accept both. What matters is meeting the entry requirements for the particular institution and course, including any required subjects and grades.

When should we get tutoring support?

Not every student needs a tutor. It may be useful when a specific subject gap is persisting, confidence is slipping, exam technique is weak or the student is struggling to organise demanding course content independently.
